Plantar Fasciitis

April 2026

← Back to Home

Plantar fasciitis is one of the most common causes of heel pain. It often presents as pain near the bottom of the heel, especially with the first few steps in the morning or after periods of rest.

Common Contributing Factors

Contributing factors may include increased standing or walking, training changes, calf tightness, reduced ankle dorsiflexion, higher body weight, and footwear that does not provide adequate support for the individual.

General Treatment Approaches

Treatment often includes activity modification, stretching, strengthening, footwear advice, taping, orthotic therapy when appropriate, and progressive loading strategies. Some cases may also involve shockwave therapy or other interventions depending on severity and duration.

Clinical Note

It is important to consider alternative diagnoses when symptoms are atypical, persistent, or associated with neurological features.

Warts

April 2026

← Back to Home

Plantar warts are skin lesions caused by human papillomavirus. They often occur on weight-bearing areas of the foot and may present with pinpoint bleeding, interruption of skin lines, and pain with side-to-side compression.

Clinical Features

Warts may appear as rough hyperkeratotic lesions and can sometimes be mistaken for calluses. Careful clinical assessment is important in differentiating these lesions.

Treatment Options

Treatment options may include salicylic acid, cantharidin-based treatments, debridement, cryotherapy, microwave therapy, and other approaches depending on the case.

Clinical Note

Persistent or atypical lesions may require further investigation or referral, especially if the diagnosis is uncertain or if standard treatment has failed.

Wounds

April 2026

← Back to Home

Foot wounds can arise from pressure, friction, trauma, vascular compromise, neuropathy, or systemic disease. Proper assessment is essential to determine healing potential and risk.

Important Assessment Factors

Assessment may include wound size and depth, tissue type, drainage, signs of infection, vascular status, sensation, pressure areas, and overall patient health.

General Management Principles

Management may involve pressure offloading, appropriate dressings, debridement when indicated, infection control, vascular assessment, and referral when higher-risk findings are present.

Clinical Note

Wounds in high-risk patients should be monitored closely, particularly when there is reduced sensation, poor circulation, or delayed healing.
